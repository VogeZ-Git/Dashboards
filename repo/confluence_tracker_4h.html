WHAT THIS IS

A daily snapshot of where every ticker on the watchlist sits relative to its Bollinger bands, and - the point of the whole thing - the exact price at which it would re-enter the band. That price is where a limit-buy or a stop goes, so you don't have to watch charts.

It is a position and order-placement aid. It is not a signal service and it is not a strategy shown to make money. Read VALIDATION before treating anything here as an edge.

THE 30-SECOND VERSION

1. The page opens on OUTSIDE - only names currently outside their band. Those are the only ones with anything to do.
2. A BELOW name is stretched to the downside. RE-ENTRY is where a limit-buy goes.
3. An ABOVE name is stretched to the upside. RE-ENTRY is where a trim or stop goes.
4. Nudge that price by DRIFT/BAR times however many days you think it takes to fill. The band moves; your resting order doesn't.

HOW THE BANDS ARE BUILT

The basis is a 90/1.7-style exponential moving average of the close. The bands sit a multiple of the standard deviation above and below. The setting is fixed at 90/1.7 - 90 bars, 1.7 deviations - matching the original Pine indicator.

One quirk is carried over deliberately: the deviation is measured around the simple moving average, not around the EMA basis. It looks wrong and it is reproduced on purpose, so these numbers match the TradingView chart exactly.

EVERY COLUMN

TICKERWatchlist symbol. A few map to a different exchange symbol behind the scenes.
CLOSELast confirmed daily close. Not intraday.
ZONEBELOW under the lower band. ABOVE over the upper band. INSIDE normal range, nothing to do.
SIGNALWhat fired on the last bar. See SIGNAL TYPES. A dash means nothing fired.
RE-ENTRYThe number you act on. The band level price must cross to generate a hard signal: the lower band when BELOW, the upper band when ABOVE. Blank when INSIDE, because there is nothing to cross.
GAP %Distance from the last close to that level, in percent.
DRIFT/BARHow far the RE-ENTRY level has moved per bar over the last five. The column people ignore and then wonder why they never get filled. A falling stock has a falling lower band; an order left at today's level is stale within a week.
BARS OUTConsecutive bars outside the band. A high number means a persistent trend, not a stretched rubber band. Treat those with suspicion.
%BPosition within the band. 0.00 is exactly on the lower band, 1.00 on the upper, 0.50 at the basis. Outside 0-1 means outside the band.
WIDTH %Band width as a percent of the basis - a volatility proxy. Narrow bands mean a quiet market and easily-triggered signals.
LOWER / BASIS / UPPERThe three band values as of the last close.
AS OFDate of the close this row was computed from. If it isn't the last trading day, that ticker's data is stale.

SIGNAL TYPES

HARD BUYPrice closed back above the lower band after being below it. The mean-reversion entry.
HARD SELLPrice closed back below the upper band after being above it. The trim.
SOFT BUYStill below the band, but the 3-bar EMA has turned up. Early warning that a hard buy may be coming. Not a confirmed entry.
SOFT SELLStill above the band, 3-bar EMA turning down.
-cdCooldown. A hard signal fired within the last 3 bars, so further signals are suppressed to stop one wobble producing a burst of trades.

THE PAIRS TAB

Every combination of two watchlist names, as a ratio A/B, with the same 90/1.7 bands applied to that ratio. It answers: which pairs have moved apart unusually far relative to their own recent history.

RE-ENTRY is the ratio level, not a price. A LEG % is how far the first ticker must move to get there with the second held still - that is the tradeable number. B LEG % is the equivalent move in the second leg. The two are not mirror images, which is why both are shown.

HALF-LIFEHow many bars it historically takes the ratio to close half the distance back to its mean, from an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck fit. Low is good. "none" means the ratio is drifting apart rather than reverting - the opposite of what a pair trade needs.
CORRCorrelation of the two tickers' daily log returns. Low correlation means the two legs aren't really related and the "spread" is just two independent things being noisy together.
-loThe pair fails the half-life or correlation filter. Shown only on the unfiltered views.

This tab needs more scepticism than the ticker tab, not less. With 39 tickers there are 741 combinations. Testing 741 things and reporting the extreme ones is exactly the multiple-comparisons trap - some will always look stretched, purely by chance.

The half-life filter is calibrated rather than guessed. On 600 pure random walks of realistic length, the estimator returns a finite half-life most of the time, because of well-known small-sample bias. A 120-bar cut passes 29% of pure noise, 80 bars passes 10%, and the 60-bar cut used here passes about 4%. Across 741 pairs that still means roughly 30 pairs clear it on noise alone. The banner at the top of the tab shows that number for your actual run.

What would make a pair real, and is not tested here: a genuine economic link between the two businesses, and a cointegration test on a longer sample. A stretched ratio plus a short half-life is a place to start looking. It is not evidence. Nothing on this tab has been validated the way the ticker settings were - and that validation, when it was run, came back negative.

THE BUY SIDE AND THE SELL SIDE ARE NOT EQUAL

Worth knowing, though the tool does nothing about it automatically: this is a mean-reversion model, and mean reversion is destroyed by strong trends. Trimming half a position every time a compounder touches the upper band is how a +2000% hold turns into a +695% one. In the backtests the sell side was where nearly all the damage came from.

So lean on the buy side. Treat an upper-band touch on something in a long-running uptrend as information, not an instruction.

VALIDATION - READ BEFORE TRADING IT

The setting is fixed at 90/1.7 on purpose. Three rounds of increasingly careful optimization were built and run, and all three failed.

Per-ticker grid search: the correlation between in-sample and out-of-sample edge was 0.10 - statistically indistinguishable from zero. The number of tickers clearing their significance threshold was exactly what chance predicts.

Pooled walk-forward fit across the whole watchlist, three rolling folds, scored only on data it had never seen: every fold was strongly positive in training and negative in testing (+30 to -40, +28 to -28, +12 to -10). The final walk-forward edge was -33.65 versus +4.46 for plain 90/1.7.

The null test: each ticker's returns were chopped into blocks and shuffled, rebuilding fake series with identical volatility and drift but no memory, then the whole optimizer was re-run on them 45 times. The fitted setting landed at the 0th percentile - worse than every single shuffled version of the same data.

Why: fitted settings chose fast, tight bands and traded roughly five times more. Since this model sells half the position into every upper-band touch, more trading means more damage in a rising market. 90/1.7 wins largely by trading less.

And 90/1.7 itself has no proven edge either - +4.46 median with 16 of 31 windows positive is a coin flip. It simply doesn't destroy value the way the fitted settings did. Treat everything here as descriptive: the bands tell you where price is stretched, not that it will revert.

How to use it anyway. Pick names on your own thesis. Use this to time entries into things you already want to own, and to place an order at a level instead of chasing. Lean on the buy side; be sceptical of the sell side, especially in anything trending up. Do not let it decide what you buy.

LIVE PRICES

The badge in the header shows whether a live price feed is attached. LIVE 15:12 means prices are current as of that time, prices 40m old means the feed has gone stale, and no live feed means the page is showing closing prices only.

Prices come from a Google Sheet of GOOGLEFINANCE formulas, published as JSON by an Apps Script web app and polled by this page. That runs in Google's cloud, so it keeps updating whether or not the PC that builds these pages is switched on. The data is delayed, typically by up to 20 minutes - fine for placing resting orders against a band level, not a trading feed.

Only the price is live. The bands are not. A band needs a completed bar, and an intraday price does not create one. So LOWER, BASIS and UPPER stay fixed at the last close, while CLOSE, ZONE, GAP %, RE-ENTRY and %B move with the price.

The SIGNAL column is deliberately frozen too. A signal is defined by a bar closing back inside the band. Recomputing it from a live price would make it appear and vanish through the session - the classic repainting trap. What fires intraday is not a signal until the bar closes.

A ticker with no live quote simply keeps its closing price. GOOGLEFINANCE does not cover every symbol Yahoo does, so expect a few of those.

TIMEFRAMES

The header shows which bars this page was built from. Two exist:

dailyFive years of daily closes. The default. A 90-bar band is about four and a half months of history.
4-hourYahoo has no native 4-hour bar, so hourly bars are fetched and grouped in fours within each session. A 6.5-hour US session gives two bars a day, so a 90-bar band is about seven weeks. Yahoo caps hourly history at 730 days.

Grouping inside the day rather than on a wall-clock grid means half-days and non-US sessions produce short final buckets instead of shifting every later bar. The last 4-hour bar of the current session is incomplete until the close, so its signal can change - the same repaint caveat as running the daily build during US hours.

The two timeframes use separate cache and output files and do not interfere.

DATA AND REFRESH

Daily closes from Yahoo via yfinance, cached locally for six hours. Prices are split-adjusted but not dividend-adjusted, to match a default TradingView chart. The page is fully static - everything was computed when the file was built, and that timestamp is in the header.

Rebuild after the US close with python confluence_tracker.py. Run it at 22:30 Amsterdam time and the last bar is final; running during US hours gives you an unconfirmed bar whose signal can vanish by the close.

Yahoo returns an empty response when it rate-limits rather than an error. The script detects that, keeps the old cache, and shows an amber banner instead of writing an empty page. --offline rebuilds from cache without touching the network.
