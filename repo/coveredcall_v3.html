// MANUAL How to use this tool
What this tool does

This is a decision engine for managing MSTR covered calls using the 10Δ strategy. Each week when you check your position, you enter three things — your current delta, your DTE, and a rough P/L — and it tells you exactly what action to take and how to execute it step by step.

The 5 inputs explained
Position Status Whether you currently have an open short call or not. If you don't, the tool skips straight to opening instructions.
Current Delta (Δ) The delta your short call is showing right now, not when you opened it. Check your broker's options chain. It updates live during market hours.
Current DTE Days to expiration remaining on your short call today. Below 30 DTE is a red flag — the tool will tell you to roll regardless of delta.
Stock Direction A rough read on whether MSTR has been going up, sideways, or down since you opened the position. Used for context in the recommendation.
P/L Slider How much of the premium you've captured so far, as a percentage. Once it reaches your chosen profit threshold, the tool suggests closing early and re-opening rather than waiting for the last few cents.
Profit Threshold The deck names 50%, 60%, 70% and 80% as equally valid profit-taking levels and declines to pick one. This selector lets you set your own; it drives the early-close recommendation.
4 Ways to Close or Manage a Position
Example A — Hold to Expiration No adjustment. If OTM at expiry → keep shares + start new 10Δ next week. If ITM → assigned (shares sold at strike + keep all premium). Assignments occur after market close.
Example B — Close Early & Keep Shares Buy-to-Close (BTC) the short call. If the contract appreciated → small loss; if depreciated → net profit. You keep your 100 shares.
Example C — Close Early & Sell Shares BTC the short call + sell shares (can be done as one transaction). You receive the net cash difference.
Example D — Roll Short Calls (Preferred) In one transaction: BTC current call + STO new 40 DTE 10Δ call. You keep shares and ideally receive a credit. Roll weekly for average IV capture.
What if the stock runs?
You probably won’t roll straight back to a new 10Δ. Instead, roll up/out to the highest strike that still gives a credit (or net zero). Match deltas closely. You will almost always receive a credit if you roll weekly at 40 DTE. Each roll pushes the “goal posts” further out. Once the stock levels off or declines, roll deltas back down (30Δ → 20Δ → 10Δ). Rolling 40 DTE weekly keeps your delta low and reduces surge risk.
What if the stock falls?
Either close the short calls early for a profit, or roll out to a new 10Δ. If the stock drops far enough that your original 10Δ has decayed to a 7Δ or 5Δ, you can roll out and down to the new 10Δ at a lower strike and collect an additional credit.
The Full Cycle

A full cycle is complete when you start with a 10Δ, roll for more than one week, and end by selling another 10Δ. In between, the delta is allowed to wander up and back down with the stock — that drift is the strategy working, not a failure of it.

If you roll roughly weekly while targeting ~40 DTE, the specific day of the week — and whether MSTR is up or down that day — makes little difference. Over many rolls you are capturing an average implied volatility and an average share price, much like dollar-cost averaging.

The 6 outcomes & what triggers them
Hold / On Target Delta is 9–13Δ and DTE is 30+. You're in the sweet spot. Check back next week.
Roll Down Delta dropped below 8Δ because the stock fell. Roll down to the new 10Δ strike and collect extra credit.
Roll Up & Out Delta is 14–22Δ. Stock is creeping up. Roll up to match delta at 40 DTE, pushing the strike further away.
Aggressive Roll Delta is 23–35Δ. Stock is running hard. Roll immediately, accept a smaller credit, and keep extending the DTE.
Emergency / Assignment Delta above 35Δ. Choose: fight to keep shares with an aggressive roll, or accept assignment and execute a buy-write the next morning.
Early Profit P/L slider at or above your chosen threshold (50–80%). Enough theta has decayed — close and re-open at 40 DTE to restart the clock.
DTE Below 30 Checked before delta. Below 30 DTE the tool tells you to roll out regardless of where delta sits, because gammafication makes a credit roll progressively harder from there.
The golden rules baked into this tool
1Always target 40 DTE when opening or rolling. Never let DTE drop below 30.
2Always use delta to find the strike — never a fixed dollar amount OTM. The 10Δ strike changes every day.
3When rolling, match the current delta at the new expiry. This almost always generates a credit.
4If a roll generates a loss, carry it forward into the new position. Time will work it out.
5Do not sell weeklies. Weeklies pay the most per week, but gammafication makes them exponentially harder to manage when the stock runs unexpectedly.
6Delta is not the probability of expiring ITM. That is a separate metric. Delta is the price change per $1 move in MSTR — nothing more.
7Strike prices for 10Δ calls are not fixed. They move continuously with IV and share price, and only update during primary market hours. Re-check the chain every time.
Gammafication — why 40 DTE, not weeklies

Gammafication is not a standard options term; it is the deck author's own word for the specific risk being avoided here: the accelerating influence of gamma on an option's delta as expiration approaches. Near expiry, even small moves in MSTR swing delta rapidly and dramatically.

Gamma is the "delta of delta." What matters in practice is the combined effect of rising gamma, ongoing theta decay, and the natural increase in gamma as DTE falls. The consequence: the closer to expiration, the harder it becomes to roll a short call out for a credit — especially once the strike goes in-the-money.

The resolution: open at 40 days out, then roll roughly weekly for a net credit, adjusting the strike as the market moves. You still collect weekly income, but you keep a built-in safety net and plenty of time to manage the trade if MSTR moves against you.
Delta ≠ Probability ITM

"Delta is roughly the chance of finishing ITM" is a common shortcut, and the deck calls it out as oversimplified and incorrect. The two numbers are related but distinct, and at low deltas the gap is material.

Delta Approximate change in the option's price per $1 move in the underlying. N(d1) in Black-Scholes.
Probability ITM Likelihood the option finishes above the strike at expiration. N(d2). For a 40 DTE 10Δ MSTR call this lands under 6% — noticeably below the 10 the delta suggests.
Prob. of Touch Likelihood the stock reaches the strike at any point during the option's life. Roughly double probability ITM. Relevant because you manage the position before expiry, not only at it.
Given MSTR's typical 80–100% IV, a 10Δ call sits roughly 1.4–1.5 standard deviations out. Held to expiration with no adjustments, the chance of finishing ITM is under 6%. Because the strategy rolls weekly — and rolls up when the stock rises — the real assignment probability from the start of a cycle is meaningfully lower still.
Reading Volatility
Implied Volatility Unique to every individual option. The IV on a $500 strike this Friday differs from the same strike two Fridays out; calls and puts at the same strike and expiry usually differ too. A single IV figure on a website is typically an average across options expiring 30/45/60 days out.
Historical Vol Actual past movement of the underlying (30/60/90-day or trailing 12 months). Higher HV means a riskier security — but risk cuts both ways and is not inherently bad.
IV Percentile Where current average IV sits against the prior 12 months. Near 100 = higher than anything in the past year; near 1 = lower. Favour buying long options when it's low, selling short options when it's high.
Ideal conditions are rare. The practical way to capture a favourable average IV is to keep rolling short contracts on a weekly basis — the volatility equivalent of dollar-cost averaging. Two strikes only $5–$10 apart on the same expiry can still differ meaningfully in IV; all else equal, higher IV gives the seller more premium.
What if you sell the 15Δ or 20Δ instead?

More premium, but ITM probability rises faster than the premium does. Sampled on live MSTR data (6 Aug 2025):

30 DTE 15Δ vs 10Δ: +50% premium, +54% ITM probability · 20Δ vs 10Δ: +123% premium, +127% ITM probability · 20Δ vs 15Δ: +48% premium, +47% ITM probability
37 DTE 15Δ vs 10Δ: +70% premium, +68% ITM probability · 20Δ vs 10Δ: +120% premium, +126% ITM probability · 20Δ vs 15Δ: +29% premium, +34% ITM probability
44 DTE 25Δ vs 10Δ: +53% premium, +63% ITM probability · 20Δ vs 10Δ: +112% premium, +131% ITM probability · 20Δ vs 15Δ: +38% premium, +41% ITM probability
Read the pairs: in most of these samples the added ITM risk grows as fast as or faster than the added premium. That's the case for starting at 10Δ — you aren't leaving free money on the table, you're declining a roughly fair trade in exchange for a far quieter position to manage.
Key Options Terminology
Intrinsic Value Real profit if exercised now. Call = stock price – strike. Example: Stock $55, strike $50 = $5 intrinsic.
Extrinsic Value Time value + volatility premium. This is what decays (theta). Key insight: extrinsic value decays as expiration approaches.
Call Options – Two Sides of the Trade
Buyer (Long Call) Right (not obligation) to buy 100 shares at strike. Pays premium. Max risk = premium paid. Needs big move up.
Seller (Short Call / Covered Call) Collects premium upfront. Profits if stock stays flat or drops. You are the income generator.
Zero-Sum Reminder: Before every trade ask: “Why would someone take the opposite side?”
Mean Reversion outside the deck

Standard Deviation (SD) measures volatility. +2 or –2 SD signals = price is overextended → higher probability of mean reversion. Applied to 10Δ rolls: when delta spikes hard, the stock is likely overextended — prepare to roll or close.

TA Exit Rules outside the deck · conflicts
Practical TA Rules (apply to MSTR):
• Mark prior swing highs after every pullback.
• If price breaks and closes above that high → strong continuation signal.
• Close the short call (BTC) immediately — even at a small loss.
• Do NOT let it run or roll in “rolling prison” during parabolic moves.
• 50% profit rule is also excellent: take it and re-open at 40 DTE 10Δ.

Example: You sold a 10Δ call for $15 premium. If the call price climbs back to $25 on a breakout → close it. A $10 loss is acceptable because you can immediately sell the next 10Δ and recover faster than holding to a $60–$120 loss.

The deck's answer to a stock that runs is the opposite: keep rolling up and out for a credit, because each roll moves the goalposts and statistically improves your odds once the stock levels off. On negative rolls it says to roll for a credit anyway and carry the losses forward into the new covered calls — the documented 6/23–8/15 run did exactly that and still finished +$11,292 (+11.7%) over 53 days. The one point both agree on is the 50% profit rule. The decision engine above follows the deck.

The Two Ways to Run This Strategy
1. Covered Calls Sell short calls against shares you own. This is the standard route and what the decision framework above assumes. 100 shares per contract.
2. PMCC Sell short calls against deep in-the-money long calls with more than 365 days to expiration (LEAPS). Formally a diagonal bull call spread, commonly called a Poor Man's Covered Call. Same 10Δ / 40 DTE discipline applies to the short leg.
⚠

PMCCs carry meaningfully higher risk and are not recommended for new options traders or anyone sensitive to large drawdowns. If an 80%+ paper loss would keep you up at night, do not trade option spreads.

The Collar — capping downside on a large position

If you hold a large MSTR position and your priority shifts to capturing upside while limiting downside, the collar is the structure. Most often used after a significant run-up, to limit large potential losses without selling the shares — which can also preserve long-term holding status rather than triggering short-term gains.

Buy 100 shares of MSTR
Sell-to-Open 1× 12Δ covered call
Buy-to-Open 1× 10Δ put
Why 12Δ on the call but 10Δ on the put? Because of natural downside skew, puts cost more than calls at equivalent deltas. Selling the call at a modestly higher delta usually generates enough premium to fully offset the cost of the protective put. Both strikes are out-of-the-money, just at different levels. Can be executed as one combined transaction or two separate trades.

If the primary goal is instead to lock in gains, sell higher-delta options on both sides — for example 50Δ calls and 50Δ puts straddling the current share price.

// CONTINGENCY The MSTR 10Δ Liquidation Contingency Plan

Critics of the 10Δ strategy often fixate on one scenario: what happens if shares get called away? To the inexperienced, assignment feels catastrophic — as if the investment is permanently lost. It isn't. This plan exists precisely for that moment. If you reach it, it means your portfolio just experienced a massive run-up. That is a good problem to have.

The plan below covers the full recovery sequence — from assignment through buy-write re-entry and the gradual return to standard 10Δ management.

Phase 1 Assignment Occurs Trigger Event
!
For assignment to occur despite active rolling, MSTR would need a sustained parabolic surge — the kind where even 40 DTE rolls can no longer generate a credit at any strike. This is statistically rare, but it does happen (e.g. the Nov–Dec 2024 MSTR spike).
1
Your 100 shares are called away at the strike price. Your account converts to cash. You keep every dollar of premium you collected across all prior rolls — that accumulated income is yours regardless.
2
Calculate your net effective exit price: Strike price received + all premium collected throughout the life of the position. This figure is your true realized gain — it will almost certainly exceed your original cost basis by a significant margin.
3
Take the weekend. A parabolic MSTR run of the magnitude required to cause assignment is a wealth accumulation event. Treat it as one.
💡
Why parabolic runs work in your favor post-assignment

When MSTR spikes hard enough to cause assignment, implied volatility (IV) spikes with it — sometimes dramatically. Elevated IV means covered call premiums are at their richest. The very event that caused your assignment also sets up your best re-entry conditions. The strategy doesn't end at assignment; it resets at a higher level.

Phase 2 Buy-Write Re-Entry Next Trading Day
1
At market open the next trading day, execute a buy-write — a single simultaneous transaction that buys 100 shares and sells a covered call in one order. Most brokers support this as a combo order.
2
Unlike your normal 10Δ open, sell a higher-delta call for this buy-write. Why? Parabolic surges rarely sustain a second consecutive month-long run. A higher delta call (20–30Δ) gives you a much larger premium, dramatically lowering your new cost basis from day one.
3
Target the strike carefully: Choose a 40 DTE call at a delta level such that the premium received brings your effective cost basis below the strike price you were just assigned at. This is your new break-even anchor.
4
Calculate your new break-even: New cost basis = Purchase price − Premium received on buy-write − All prior premiums collected. This number will be lower than you expect.
// NEW COST BASIS FORMULA
New cost basis = Repurchase price − Buy-write premium − All prior premiums collected
The accumulated premium from months of theta decay means your effective cost basis on the new position is significantly lower than the raw repurchase price suggests.
Phase 3 Normalize Back to 10Δ Weeks 2 – 6+
1
After the buy-write, resume weekly position checks using the standard framework above. With a higher-delta starting position, the delta will naturally decay as the stock stabilizes.
2
MSTR historically consolidates after parabolic surges — exactly as it did from December 2024 through March 2025. Consolidation means theta works for you while delta drifts back down toward the 10Δ target zone.
3
Each week, use the decision framework to roll back down: 30Δ → 25Δ → 20Δ → 15Δ → 10Δ. Each roll generates credit. Each roll lowers the effective strike. The normalization process itself is profitable.
4
Once you're back to 10Δ at 40 DTE, you're fully restored to standard operating mode — but with a lower cost basis, more premium in the bank, and the experience of having executed the contingency plan successfully.

The right mental model

Without this plan, assignment feels like a disaster. With it, assignment is just a speed bump — a temporary detour that happens to come with a large cash deposit. The skeptics who dismiss covered call strategies rarely have a plan for what comes next. You do. The math has always been on your side. The contingency plan simply makes that explicit.
